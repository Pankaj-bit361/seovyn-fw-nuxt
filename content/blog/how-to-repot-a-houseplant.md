Roots growing through drainage holes are a reason to inspect a houseplant, but they do not automatically call for a much bigger pot. A plant with room around its roots and a salty crust on its soil may need fresh mix instead of more space. This guide explains how to repot a houseplant by making that choice first, then handling the roots and aftercare carefully.

To repot a houseplant, inspect its roots and soil before choosing a container. Use a slightly larger draining pot when the roots need space, or refresh the mix in the existing pot when they do not. Ease out the root ball, address circling roots, replant at the same depth, water, and let the pot drain.

## How to tell whether your houseplant needs repotting

Start with evidence, not an annual reminder. The [University of Arkansas Extension](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/counties/white/news/horticulture/20210428RepottingHouseplants.aspx) says most houseplants need repotting only once every three to five years. That is a description of typical frequency, not a schedule to follow regardless of the plant’s condition.

Look for a combination of these signs:

- **Roots need space.** Roots growing through drainage holes or a root ball crowded against the pot wall suggest that the plant may be pot-bound. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/repotting-houseplants/) identifies roots emerging through drainage holes and pot-bound roots as reasons a larger container may become necessary.
- **Growth has changed.** Small new leaves and almost no new growth are signs identified by Arkansas Extension. Check whether the plant is getting suitable light and water before assuming its pot is the cause.
- **The soil needs attention.** Arkansas Extension recommends repotting when a salty crust appears on the soil surface and replacing as much affected soil as possible without excessive root disturbance.
- **The plant is unstable or dries unusually quickly.** A top-heavy plant that tips over, particularly when dry, or one that has begun drying out between its usual waterings may need inspection.

If the plant is growing well, its pot is stable, and the mix remains in good condition, leave it alone. If the signs point to a problem, slide out the root ball before deciding whether the remedy is a larger pot or a soil refresh.

## When to repot—and when to wait

The seasonal guidance is best understood as a window around renewed growth, not one mandatory date. Arkansas Extension recommends **late winter**, as natural light increases and houseplants begin waking from slower winter growth. For a Seattle home, late winter into early spring is a practical time to assess plants as daylight increases. A plant with an immediate root or soil problem need not wait for that window.

A newly purchased houseplant is not automatically ready for another change. Place it where its light and temperature needs can be met, observe how the mix dries, and inspect for a clear reason to repot. If it is healthy in its nursery pot, let it adjust to the home before disturbing its roots. There is no single waiting period that fits every purchase; a damaged pot, severe crowding, or deteriorating mix changes the decision.

This pause also makes diagnosis easier. Wilting shortly after a move may reflect a change in light, temperature, or watering rather than a need for a larger container. Repotting before identifying the problem can obscure the cause and add root disturbance.

## Choose the right pot and potting mix

If the roots need more space, choose a pot roughly **1–2 inches wider in diameter** than the current one. The goal is an incremental increase, not enough empty space for years of anticipated growth. Arkansas Extension likewise recommends increasing pot size gradually and notes that a larger root system generally supports a larger plant.

Use a planting pot with drainage holes and place a saucer beneath it to catch runoff. A decorative pot without holes can serve as an outer container for a removable, draining inner pot. After watering, take the inner pot out or check beneath it so it does not sit in collected water. An oversized pot is harder to manage because a substantial volume of mix can remain wet beyond the roots.

Select potting mix for the type of plant rather than using soil dug from the garden. A general-purpose indoor mix is a starting point for many foliage houseplants; plants sold for specialized growing conditions, such as orchids or cacti, need a mix appropriate to their roots and moisture requirements. Check a bag’s ingredients and feeding information before use. A product labeled organic, including an Espoma Organic mix, still needs to suit the particular plant.

Pot material affects how quickly the mix may dry. An unglazed clay pot can lose moisture through its sides, while a plastic pot retains it more readily. Either can work when it has drainage and watering is adjusted to the conditions in the home.

## How to repot a houseplant step by step

Set a tray or newspaper on a washable surface before opening the potting mix. Gather the chosen container, fresh mix, gloves or a hand trowel, clean pruning shears, a watering can, and a saucer. Keep stems clear of the work area’s edge so they are not bent as the pot is turned.

### Remove and inspect the root ball

1. **Ease the plant out.** Tip the container sideways while supporting the base of the plant and the root ball. Squeeze a flexible plastic pot or work around its inside edge with a trowel if needed. Do not lift the plant by its leaves or pull hard on its stems.
2. **Check the roots and remaining mix.** Look for roots circling the outside, a dense mass at the bottom, and mix that falls away or remains between roots. This inspection determines whether to pot up or refresh the mix in the same container.
3. **Address circling or dead roots.** Gently loosen outer roots with fingertips. Arkansas Extension advises cutting circling roots and removing dead, sloughing roots during repotting. Use clean shears where a root cannot be redirected without tearing it; do not cut healthy roots merely to make the root ball look tidy.

**Do you remove all the old soil?** No. Let loose mix fall away, but do not strip sound mix from every root during an ordinary repot. If a salty crust is the reason for the work, replace as much affected soil as practical without overly disturbing the root system, as Arkansas Extension advises.

### Set the plant at the correct depth

4. **Add a base of fresh mix.** Put enough in the new or cleaned existing pot to hold the root ball at its previous planting depth. Test the height before filling the sides; the soil surface should remain below the rim so there is room to water.
5. **Fill around the roots.** Center the plant and add mix in small amounts around the root ball. Work around the full circumference, especially where the roots meet the pot wall, to avoid leaving large empty gaps.
6. **Settle the mix lightly.** Tap the pot gently and press the surface with fingertips just enough to support the plant. Add mix where it settles, but do not pack it hard or bury the stem more deeply than it was before.

## Watering, feeding, and adjustment after repotting

Water after planting to moisten fresh mix and settle it around the root ball. Allow water to leave through the drainage holes, then empty the saucer after runoff stops. If the pot sits inside a decorative outer container, check that container as well; hidden standing water defeats the benefit of a draining inner pot.

Do not feed immediately to try to force new growth. Fresh potting mixes differ in their nutrient content, so read the bag label before deciding when fertilizer is needed. During the plant’s adjustment, check moisture in the mix before watering again rather than carrying over its old watering schedule unchanged.

Watch the direction of change over the following days. A plant can pause visibly after its roots have been handled, but persistent wilting calls for a closer look. Feel the mix and check drainage before adding more water: wet roots and dry roots require different responses. Keep the plant in suitable light rather than repeatedly moving it while it settles.

## Larger pot or same-pot refresh? A practical comparison

**Potting up** specifically means moving a plant into a larger container. **Repotting** is broader: it can also mean removing a plant to replace mix and returning it to the same pot. The choice follows from what inspection reveals, not from a rule that every repot must increase container size.

Consider a plant in a 6-inch pot. If roots densely circle the root ball and the plant dries unusually quickly, a draining pot around 7–8 inches wide gives those roots modest additional space. If the roots are not crowded but the soil has a salty crust, a cleaned 6-inch pot and fresh mix address the soil without adding unnecessary volume.

| Inspection finding | Better starting choice | What the choice addresses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crowded, circling roots and little room in the current container | A slightly larger draining pot | Space for continued root growth |
| Roots have room, but the mix has a salty crust | Fresh mix in the cleaned existing pot | A soil problem without unnecessary pot enlargement |
| Plant is healthy in a recently purchased nursery pot | Leave it in place for now | Avoiding an unneeded disturbance during adjustment |
| Plant is top-heavy but roots are not crowded | Assess stability and pot shape before increasing size | Support without assuming more root space is needed |

Keeping a plant in the same pot can help limit its eventual size. Root pruning is sometimes used for that purpose, but it is more disruptive than replacing loose, spent mix. For a beginner, a same-pot refresh is not a reason to cut an otherwise healthy root system.

## FAQ

### What if roots have grown through the drainage holes?

Support the root ball and inspect how firmly the roots are caught before pulling the plant free. Do not force thick roots back through narrow holes or tug on the stems. A small number of exposed roots is a prompt to inspect the whole root ball; Penn State Extension identifies roots emerging through drainage holes as one sign that a larger container may become necessary.

### What if the root ball falls apart while repotting?

Place the remaining rooted sections together in the pot at their previous depth and add fresh mix around them carefully. Avoid repeated lifting to reconstruct a perfect ball: that can disturb more roots. Water to settle the mix, confirm that the container drains, and then give the plant stable light and a chance to recover before considering further changes.

### Can a top-heavy houseplant stay in the same size pot?

Yes, if its roots still have room. Check whether a wider-based container, a heavier outer pot, or a more stable location solves the tipping problem before increasing soil volume. Arkansas Extension lists top-heaviness as a reason to assess repotting, particularly when a plant becomes dry, but stability and root crowding are separate findings.

### Should a houseplant that stops growing in winter be repotted?

Not for that reason alone. Compare its leaves, roots, soil condition, and watering behavior before deciding. Arkansas Extension recommends late winter for planned repotting because increasing natural light coincides with renewed growth. A plant with sound mix and uncrowded roots can remain in its pot while seasonal growth is slow.
